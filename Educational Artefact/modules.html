Learning modules

Build your privacy instinct.

Begin with the basics, then test your judgement with realistic scenarios. You can complete the modules in any order.

01

What counts as information?

Separate obvious identifiers, sensitive information and digital traces. Learn why a collection of small details can identify someone.

Try the challenge
  1. A school uniform, badge or timetable can reveal where someone studies and when they may be there.
  2. A username combined with a birth month and sports team may identify a person even without their full name.
  3. A photo’s location metadata can reveal where it was taken, even when the image itself looks harmless.
  4. Several public posts can reveal a routine: home area, transport route, favourite café and usual time online.
  5. A device ID, IP address or browser fingerprint may distinguish a user even when they do not type their name.

Key lesson: Information becomes identifying when separate clues are combined. Treat context and metadata as information too.

02

Decide before you share

Use purpose, consent, audience and retention to decide whether information should be collected or posted.

Try the challenge
  1. A class wants to celebrate a learner’s achievement. Confirm the learner is comfortable before posting their name and photo.
  2. A club collects phone numbers for updates. Explain the purpose, limit access and delete numbers when the activity ends.
  3. A shared document is set to “anyone with the link”. Change it to named viewers if it contains marks, contact details or drafts.
  4. A friend sends a private message and asks you to repost it. Ask permission first; a message sent to you is not automatically public.
  5. A survey asks for a full name but only needs age range and general feedback. Remove the unnecessary identifier.

Key lesson: Check purpose, consent, audience and retention. A good reason to collect information does not mean every detail is necessary.

03

AI without oversharing

Learn how to use AI tools while protecting confidential work, personal details and other people’s information.

Try the challenge
  1. Replace names, student numbers and exact dates with neutral labels before requesting a summary.
  2. Do not paste passwords, access codes or private messages into a chatbot, even if the tool appears helpful.
  3. Ask whether the AI service is approved by your school or organisation before using confidential information.
  4. Use a short fictional example to test a prompt instead of uploading a real disciplinary, medical or family record.
  5. Check AI output for invented facts, unfair assumptions and personal details before sharing it with anyone.

Key lesson: Minimise data, use approved tools and keep human responsibility for the final decision.

04

Respond to a privacy incident

Recognise a mistake, limit further exposure, preserve useful evidence and report it through a trusted channel.

Try the challenge
  1. You send a file to the wrong person. Recall it if possible, avoid further forwarding and tell the responsible supervisor promptly.
  2. You notice a public link containing contact details. Record the link privately, ask the owner to restrict access and report it through the correct channel.
  3. Your account shows an unfamiliar login. Change the password from the official site, enable multi-factor authentication and report the event.
  4. A friend’s private photo is circulating. Do not download or forward it; encourage them to report the post and seek trusted support.
  5. A lost phone contains signed-in accounts. Use the account provider’s device controls to lock or sign out, then notify the relevant organisation.

Key lesson: Contain the incident, preserve only necessary evidence, report quickly and never increase the audience while trying to help.

Information awareness toolkit

20 points to remember

Use these quick reminders when you collect, create, share or store information. Each point connects to a question in the quiz below.

01

Personal data

Names, contact details and identification numbers can identify a person.

02

Sensitive data

Health, financial, biometric and disciplinary details need extra care.

03

Digital traces

Searches, clicks, device IDs and browsing patterns can reveal behaviour.

04

Context matters

A school logo, uniform or location can identify someone without a name.

05

Metadata

Photos and documents may contain hidden location, author or time details.

06

Purpose limitation

Use information only for the clear purpose explained to the person.

07

Data minimisation

Collect the smallest amount of information needed to achieve the goal.

08

Informed consent

Consent should be clear, voluntary and based on enough information.

09

Audience control

Choose named viewers or a limited audience instead of public-by-default sharing.

10

Retention

Decide how long information is needed and delete it safely afterwards.

11

Strong passwords

Use long, unique passwords rather than reusing one across accounts.

12

Multi-factor security

A second sign-in factor can block access even if a password is stolen.

13

Phishing signals

Urgency, unexpected links and password requests deserve independent checking.

14

App permissions

Review whether an app really needs access to your camera, microphone or contacts.

15

Public networks

Avoid sensitive transactions on untrusted Wi-Fi and use secure connections.

16

Approved AI tools

Check whether an AI service is approved before using confidential information.

17

AI minimisation

Use fictional examples or placeholders instead of real personal details.

18

Human review

Verify AI outputs for errors, bias, invented facts and exposed information.

19

Incident response

Contain mistakes quickly, report them and do not forward exposed information.

20

Digital dignity

Protect other people’s images, messages, stories and choices as carefully as your own.
