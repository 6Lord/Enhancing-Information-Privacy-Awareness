Lesson 1 · Understand the information flow

A prompt is information you are sharing

A generative AI assistant uses the prompt and any supplied context to produce an answer. The service has to process what you send. What happens afterwards depends on the provider, the product, your account and its settings. Do not assume a chatbot is private just because the conversation feels personal.

Explore how information moves through an AI tool

1. You enter information

A prompt can contain names, client or colleague details, a work document, a photo, a voice recording or personal information. Uploaded material may reveal more than you intended.

2. The service processes it

The provider’s terms and settings govern handling. Prompts may be logged or retained for service, safety or improvement purposes; check the tool’s current privacy information rather than guessing.

3. AI creates an output

An answer can be inaccurate, biased or unexpectedly repeat details from your prompt. Treat generated text as something to review—not as proof that the information is safe or correct.

Important:

PromptSmart does not know the privacy settings of every AI product. If you cannot confirm that a tool is permitted and appropriate for the information, do not enter personal, sensitive or confidential details. Check your employer’s AI or data policy, or ask your manager, IT team or privacy contact.

Lesson 2 · A practical decision routine

Use P.A.U.S.E. before you prompt

Take a few seconds to check the information and the tool before asking AI for help.

Open the five-step P.A.U.S.E. guide
P

Personal?

Does the text, file or image identify you or someone else? Look for names, faces, account numbers, contact details, location, financial information and sensitive life details.

A

Approved?

Is this specific AI tool allowed by your employer for this task? Check workplace policies and the provider’s privacy settings and terms. For personal use, decide whether the service is suitable for the information.

U

Useful and necessary?

Does the AI need this detail to help? Remove anything that is not needed; use a fictional example or a neutral placeholder instead.

S

Safe to share?

If you are unsure whether the tool can handle the information appropriately, stop and check with your manager, IT team or privacy contact. Never submit passwords, access codes or another person’s private records.

E

Examine the output

Check facts, fairness and privacy before using or sharing the answer. Keep a person responsible for important decisions.

Worked example: draft a customer reply without sharing customer data

Workplace example

Get writing help without exposing a customer record

Risky prompt: “Write a reply to this customer,” followed by the customer’s full name, account number, contact details and complete complaint history.

Safer prompt: “Draft a professional, empathetic reply acknowledging a delayed delivery and explaining that I will check the status. Do not invent a resolution.”

The safer version describes the writing task without sharing customer identifiers or confidential history. Use a work-approved AI service for work tasks, provide only the necessary context, and check the draft for accuracy and policy compliance before sending. For personal tasks, apply the same minimisation principle to your own and other people’s details.

Guided practice

Apply the lessons to realistic adult situations

Choose a topic to open its explanation and challenges. Read each key lesson first, then think through situations from work and everyday life and compare your decisions.

01What can an AI prompt reveal?Recognise direct, sensitive and indirect identifiers in text, files, images and recordings.
Try the challenge
  1. You ask AI to summarise a customer case that includes a name, account number and contact details. Which details are unnecessary for summarising?
  2. A screenshot has no names but shows an employer logo, a unique role and a client issue. Could those clues identify people or the organisation?
  3. A voice note includes a colleague discussing a personal family problem. Is a transcription request a reason to share it?
  4. A document contains author names, comments and revision history. What should be checked before uploading it?
  5. A prompt describes a person’s rare circumstances without using their name. Could colleagues or friends still recognise them?

Key lesson: Names are only one clue. Job roles, client context, voice, document details and combined facts can also reveal identity.

02Choose the tool and limit the dataCheck whether the AI service is suitable, then share only what the task requires.
Try the challenge
  1. Your employer has not approved a new chatbot. What should you check or ask before using it for a work task?
  2. A privacy setting says chats are not used to improve the model. Does that by itself explain all data handling?
  3. AI is asked to suggest a weekly meal plan. Can general preferences answer the question without a real health record?
  4. A prompt contains a colleague’s name and personal story, neither of which is needed. How can you reduce identifying details?
  5. A service requests access to files or contacts for a simple text question. Is that access necessary for the task?

Key lesson: Check the tool, purpose, terms and workplace policy. Minimise what you share; one privacy setting is not a complete guarantee.

03Use AI without oversharingProtect confidential work and personal information while getting useful help.
Try the challenge
  1. Replace client names, account numbers and exact dates with neutral labels before requesting a summary.
  2. Do not paste passwords, authentication codes or private messages into a chatbot, even if the tool appears helpful.
  3. Ask whether the AI service is approved by your employer before using confidential work information.
  4. Use a fictional example to explore a personal finance question instead of uploading a real bank statement.
  5. Write a prompt that asks for help drafting a customer reply without including the customer’s identity or full case history.

Key lesson: Minimise data, use an appropriate tool and keep human responsibility for the final decision.

04Check AI outputs and respond safelyReview for errors, unfair assumptions and exposed information, and know how to report a mistake.
Try the challenge
  1. An AI summary invents a product detail for a customer reply. How can you verify it before sending?
  2. An AI tool labels a job applicant as unreliable from a short profile. Should that judgement guide a decision?
  3. An answer repeats a colleague’s private detail from a prompt. What should you avoid doing with the output?
  4. You pasted client information into a tool by mistake. Which service controls and responsible workplace contact can you use?
  5. You receive an AI-generated answer that sounds certain but is not supported. Who is responsible for verifying it?

Key lesson: AI can be inaccurate, biased or reveal prompt details. Do not circulate exposed information; report incidents and keep a person accountable.

Review and recall

20 privacy and AI reminders

Use these reminders to revisit the lessons. The quiz focuses on applying them to generative AI at work and in everyday adult life.

Show all 20 reminders
01

Personal data

Names, contact details, account numbers and identifiers can identify a person.

02

Sensitive data

Health, financial, biometric, employment and disciplinary details need extra care.

03

Digital traces

Searches, clicks, device IDs and browsing patterns can reveal behaviour.

04

Context matters

An employer logo, job title, location or distinctive case can identify someone without a name.

05

Metadata

Photos and documents may contain hidden location, author or time details.

06

Purpose limitation

Use information only for the clear purpose explained to the person.

07

Data minimisation

Collect the smallest amount of information needed to achieve the goal.

08

Permission and expectations

Respect people’s choices and applicable rules; do not assume you can share someone else’s information.

09

Audience control

Choose named viewers or a limited audience instead of public-by-default sharing.

10

Retention

Follow retention rules and delete personal or work information securely when no longer needed.

11

Strong passwords

Use long, unique passwords rather than reusing one across accounts.

12

Multi-factor security

A second sign-in factor can block access even if a password is stolen.

13

Phishing signals

Urgency, unexpected links and password requests deserve independent checking.

14

App permissions

Review whether an app really needs access to your camera, microphone or contacts.

15

Public networks

Avoid sensitive transactions on untrusted Wi-Fi and use secure connections.

16

Appropriate AI tools

Check workplace policy and service terms before using confidential information; assess personal tools carefully too.

17

AI minimisation

Use fictional examples or placeholders instead of real personal details.

18

Human review

Verify AI outputs for errors, bias, invented facts and exposed information.

19

Incident response

Contain mistakes quickly, report them through the right workplace or service channel, and do not forward exposed information.

20

Digital dignity

Protect other people’s images, messages, stories and choices as carefully as your own.
